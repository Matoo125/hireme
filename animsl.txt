Realistic near-term `progress is fewer animals in the worst conditions, slower growth in the fastest-growing markets, and building the technology and policy groundwork for a longer horizon.

Over 3,000 companies worldwide have pledged cage-free or crate-free sourcing, and cost-effectiveness estimates suggest between 10 and 160 birds affected per dollar, though the evidence base is small. Implementation is the weak point: cage-free pledge follow-through is estimated at only 48–84%. On the policy side, the Commission's July 2026 Livestock Strategy commits to proposals to ban cages for hens and broilers in 2026 and for sows by mid-2027, after a missed 2023 promise. Proposals still need to pass Parliament and member states, and past EU cage transitions took a decade or more.

. Global plant-based retail sales reached $28.9B in 2025 (+3%); plant-based meat alone was $6.6B, up 4% after inflation, led by Europe. In the US, plant-based meat and seafood sales were about $1.0B, and only 11% of households bought any in 2025. Cultivated meat is in worse shape commercially: investment is at a seven-year low, with closures including Believer Meats and Meatable. Commercial sales are currently permitted only in Singapore, the US and Australia. Politically, at least seven US states have banned it, and the EU agreed in March 2026 to restrict "meat" and related terms for alternative proteins. Public funding is also thin: government investment is no more than about 10% of the estimated $10.1B annual need. Past forecasts of near-term collapse in animal agriculture (e.g. RethinkX's 2035 projections, from memory) look very optimistic now. Better bets seem to be whole-food/protein-forward plant products and hybrids, while fermentation and public R&D are longer-run options.

OECD-FAO projects meat consumption growth of 47.9 Mt over the decade to 2034, with per capita consumption stagnating in some high-income countries. Their 2026–2035 outlook has poultry alone adding 29 Mt (+20%). This matters for animals specifically: shifting from beef to chicken cuts emissions but increases the number of animals killed and suffering per calorie. Growth is concentrated in middle-income countries, so the frontier is increasingly outside Europe and North America

Vegan health: what the evidence supports

The mainstream position of dietetic bodies (e.g. the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics) is that well-planned vegan diets can be adequate at all life stages, though some European bodies are more cautious about pregnancy and childhood. The evidence is mostly prospective cohorts, which are vulnerable to confounding, plus short RCTs showing improved LDL, blood pressure and weight.

The most informative cohort is EPIC-Oxford. Vegans there had lower risks of diabetes, diverticular disease and cataracts, but a higher risk of fractures. The hip fracture hazard ratio for vegans was 2.31 versus meat-eaters. About half the vegans studied had blood B12 levels indicating deficiency, which the authors flagged as a possible contributor to other risks. The authors also noted that how far these risks can be mitigated by good food choices, fortification and supplementation is not yet known. Baseline data were collected in 1993–2001, so today's better-supplemented vegans may do better, but that remains unproven. Elevated haemorrhagic stroke risk in vegetarians is also uncertain: a Taiwanese cohort found lower stroke incidence among vegetarians.

Practically, this means supplementing B12 without exception, and paying deliberate attention to vitamin D, iodine, calcium, long-chain omega-3s, iron, zinc and selenium. Getting adequate protein and keeping bone-loading exercise in the mix is also sensible given the fracture signal. Health is a defensible but secondary reason to be vegan: the evidence supports "can be done well," not "clearly superior."

Strongest reasons to go vegan
Scale and severity. Roughly 80+ billion land animals and probably over a trillion fish are killed annually. Most land animals are in intensive systems involving confinement, fast-growth genetics and mutilations without pain relief.
Sentience evidence. The scientific mainstream has moved toward taking vertebrate (and likely some invertebrate) sentience seriously, e.g. the 2012 Cambridge Declaration and the 2024 New York Declaration. Under any reasonable moral uncertainty, the interests at stake are large.
No necessity. In affluent countries, the pleasure and convenience of animal products must be weighed against severe suffering, and this argument works across Singer-style utilitarian and rights-based frameworks.
Environment. Poore and Nemecek's 2018 analysis found animal products use most farmland while supplying a minority of calories, with large land-use, biodiversity and emissions costs.
Public health externalities. Antimicrobial resistance and zoonotic/pandemic risk (e.g. avian influenza) are additional harms.

The main counterarguments come from people who assign much lower moral weight to animals, who see higher-welfare animal farming as permissible, or who point to trade-offs such as crop-harvest deaths or economic effects on smallholders.

Theories of change
Individual conversion and norm change. Persuade people one at a time, aiming for a cultural tipping point. Evidence for it is weak: Animal Charity Evaluators tentatively judge veg*n pledges among the less promising interventions, since participants may have cut back anyway.

Incremental welfare reform. Corporate and legislative campaigns raise standards and thereby raise the cost of animal products. Advocates debate complacency versus momentum, but the available evidence suggests reforms tend to build momentum rather than complacency.

Technology substitution. Make alternatives cheaper and tastier, so change doesn't depend on moral persuasion.
Policy and institutions. Public R&D funding, subsidy reform, procurement rules and carbon pricing.
Moral circle expansion and legal status. Long-run work on sentience science and legal standing for animals.

Most serious analysts favor a portfolio, since the approaches are complementary: welfare rules raise conventional prices, which helps alternatives compete.
